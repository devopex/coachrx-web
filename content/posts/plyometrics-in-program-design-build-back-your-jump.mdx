A strong squat or deadlift doesn't automatically mean you can sprint, change direction, or land from a jump with confidence. **Plyometrics** help bridge the gap between controlled gym strength and the fast, unpredictable movements life and sport demand.

For many adults, the goal isn't a bigger box jump. It's staying capable enough to play a sport, run with their kids, or move quickly without a hamstring, knee, or back issue getting in the way.

### Why reactive strength belongs in training

Plyometrics and ballistics train reactive strength, the ability to take in force and quickly produce force in response. That happens when you land, cut, catch yourself, jump, or push off the ground while running.

Plyometrics usually include jumps, hops, skips, and similar foot-to-ground movements. Ballistics are throws, passes, and tosses. Both use quick loading and force production, but they apply it differently.

This work matters because fitness has to carry outside the gym. Someone may deadlift 500 pounds yet struggle to pick something up from the floor comfortably or sprint without tweaking a hamstring. Strength still matters, but the body also needs regular practice moving through space, absorbing force, and responding fast.

For athletes and lifestyle clients alike, this is part of antifragility. Training shouldn't only prepare someone for a controlled barbell lift. It should help them handle the physical surprises that come with sports, recreation, work, and daily life.

> **A jump is a skill. Before asking someone to jump onto a box, teach them how to land, absorb force, and trust the position.**

### Plyometrics offer a lower-complexity speed-strength option

Olympic lifts can train rapid force production, triple extension through the ankles, knees, and hips, and coordination under load. However, the clean and jerk and snatch require substantial technical practice. In some settings, especially team training or general fitness, that learning curve may not fit the goal.

Plyometric progressions can train many related qualities with less complexity. A depth drop into a jump, for example, teaches an athlete to load, extend rapidly, and land in a controlled position. That pattern has overlap with the tension, extension, and re-tension required in Olympic lifting.

On the strength-speed continuum, Olympic lifting sits closer to speed-strength than heavy squats or deadlifts. Plyometric work can move even further toward fast force production, especially when ground contact times become short.

That doesn't mean plyometrics replace every Olympic lift. Instead, they give coaches another way to train power and movement quality. They also make a useful preparation piece before weightlifting, since they can wake up the nervous system without requiring a long technical session.

### Use the four-quadrant model to choose progressions

A simple way to organize plyometrics is by ground contact time and rate of force production. Longer contact times give a person more time to organize their body. Shorter contact times demand a faster response. ‍

The first and fourth categories often work well for learning. A squat jump gives someone time to move through a larger range of motion. Banded pogos reduce load while allowing them to practice quick contact with the floor.

Depth drops fit the absorption category. The athlete steps from a low platform, lands in a stable position, and holds it. This teaches the body to accept force before it tries to redirect that force into a jump.

Repeated pogos and hurdle hops are more demanding. They require brief ground contact, stiffness through the ankle and foot, and a fast push back into the air. Coaches should treat these as a progression, not a starting point.

### Place low-level plyometrics early in the session

Speed training works best when the athlete is fresh. Put low-level plyometrics after a general warm-up and before heavy strength work, conditioning, or a technical Olympic-lifting session.

A short prep block can raise body temperature, build energy, and help an athlete find the positions needed later in the workout. It can also give clients room to make small mistakes without fatigue turning those mistakes into ugly reps.

Low-level options include pogos, A-skips, lateral shuffles, and simple heel-rock drills. They keep the dose manageable while maintaining the springiness needed for running and jumping.

A general warm-up might lead into two or three rounds of 20 to 30 seconds of banded pogos, banded single-leg pogos, butt kicks, and lateral shuffles. The goal is crisp, small movements, not exhaustion.

Heavier athletes need thoughtful volume because every landing carries more stress. That doesn't rule out plyometrics. It simply means starting with lower heights, fewer contacts, more assistance, or a basic drill such as rocking from the heels to the toes.

### Build from banded pogos to controlled jumps

Banded pogos are a useful entry point because assistance helps someone learn the pattern with less load. The athlete presses the toes into the ground, rebounds quickly, and avoids turning the drill into a deep knee bend or squat.

Progress by changing one variable at a time. First, increase a 20-second interval toward 30 seconds. Next, use less band tension while keeping the same quality. Over several weeks, the athlete can move toward bodyweight pogos, then eventually use added resistance when it fits their experience and goals.

Depth drops follow a similar path. Begin with a low platform and prioritize a quiet, balanced landing. As confidence and control improve, build toward a 12-to-16-inch box. Higher isn't automatically better, especially when landing mechanics break down.

After a solid depth drop, add a jump. The athlete drops, lands, and immediately jumps, shortening the time between ground contact and takeoff. A depth drop into a broad jump adds full hip extension and horizontal force. Later progressions may include a change of direction after landing.

The order matters. Landing confidence gives people a destination for the movement. Without it, they often hesitate, move slowly, or avoid full hip extension because they aren't sure where or how they will finish.

### Keep the ability to run and jump

People often lose the capacity to run and jump long before they lose the desire to do either. A first CrossFit class can reveal this quickly when someone sees a box jump and realizes they haven't left the ground in years.

Running a 5K or jumping onto a low platform deserves celebration because **basic physical capacity is worth reclaiming**. Still, coaches shouldn't assume a client already knows how to run, land, or jump well. Those are practiced skills.

One coaching story captures the point. A client who felt scared to jump began with a 45-pound bumper plate. Small increases in height built confidence over several sessions. Eventually, she checked that the plate stack matched the height of a box, then made the box jump herself.

That result wasn't about a flashy performance. It was a client recovering trust in her own body through a clear, patient progression.

### Put the progression into a complete program

Plyometric work becomes more useful when it supports the rest of the session. On an upper-body strength day, lower-body jumps and landing drills can fit well in the prep work. On a lower-body day, coaches may use throws and other ballistic drills to avoid piling too much impact onto the legs.

Ballistic work, including throws, passes, and tosses, follows the same reactive-strength idea and can add power training without another complex barbell lift. It also gives athletes a chance to move explosively in different directions.

### Build skill before intensity

The ability to jump, land, run, and react quickly doesn't need to disappear with age. It needs the same steady practice as a squat, deadlift, or pull-up.

Start with simple, low-impact drills. Build confidence in the landing. Then increase speed, height, or resistance only when the movement stays controlled. **Reactive strength** is built through repeatable progressions, not by asking someone to prove it on the first rep.
