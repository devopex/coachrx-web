How much protein does she actually need? Should he eat before or after the session? Two meals or three? What about the supplement stack he read about last week?

Every coach fields these. In episode 45 of Frameworks, Carl Hardwick and James FitzGerald give nutrition a full conversation of its own after covering exercise and behavior truths the week before. They arrive at two truths worth naming, then decide there is no third one worth digging into. Everything else is downstream of those two.

### Every Nutrition Lie Sits Under the Same Umbrella

Asked what he would call nutrition lies, James did not bother listing them individually. Counting calories, diets, the quality versus quantity debate, macros, supplements. There are too many lies inside each of those to count, and they all share one umbrella: you either need to be fixed, or you need to perform, and nutrition is the lever.

That is why an authoritative number lands so well. Someone says it has to be 110 grams of protein per day and human interest piques immediately, because the person hearing it has already been told, from a young age and from every direction, that they are broken or behind. The result is chaos, and a great deal of money and career built on top of it.

James described the version of that career he finds sad. Good intentions, an entire working life spent at a desk on how do we fix this thing, when the parallel path was available the whole time: look at the principles instead.

### The First Lie Is That Behavior and Nutrition Are Separate

Carl named one lie in particular: that your behaviors can be detached from your nutritional practices. You can separate them, but doing so is fruitless. There is a decision to be made, a rhythm to be built, and a life to be lived, and behavior touches all of it.

Detach the two and watch where the head goes. Straight to objectivity. Calories, protein, carbohydrate, fat, fiber, vitamins, minerals. Hit this number because someone said it was optimal for me.

Carl's alternative is a definition, not a number. A solid fueling foundation is three questions. Are you fueling for the activity you do? For the cognition you need and want? For the energy you require across the day? Everything else is secondary to those.

James agreed and pointed out the same is true of movement, which raises the obvious question of why they keep separating behavior, exercise, and nutrition at all. Because the audience is practitioners, and pulling each one into its own bucket to dig is how a coach builds depth. The words he uses to bridge behavior and nutrition are knowledge acquisition, awareness, and intuition, pushed forward into will and then choice. Those are not lunges and broccoli. That is the behavior realm: how a person thinks, how they spend their time, what stories they live with every day.

### Truth One: Eat Real Food

James framed it through a real conversation. He is in Coeur d'Alene with a hockey buddy who wants to know the most important thing to consider about his fueling. The man expects protein timing, clinical journals, and supplementation for old guys who probably should not be playing hockey. James does not go there. He goes to what real food is, individualized to the person in front of him, informed by eyes, posture, how the man approached him, who else is in the room. Twenty years ago he would have started where the guy's reading had left him and worked him backward. It was exhausting. Over time he landed on the simplicity instead.

To the public, real food is food without much of an ingredient list. If it comes bagged, the fewest ingredients possible, and they are there so it can sit for a few days.

To a coach, he framed it as the biological realm of sustenance, nurturing, survival, and reproduction. Birds in the air, animals in the water, animals on land, and anything the land grows: fruit, vegetables, peas, nuts, seeds, grains. A few weeks ago he went foraging for mushrooms with a friend who knows more about it than he does, then chopped them with onions to go alongside a steak. That is the entire concept.

### The Proof He Will Never Have

Carl pushed where the argument actually gets pushed. There is science suggesting real food does not serve you much better than what we would call fake food, provided macronutrients and calories are accounted for.

James's answer was more honest than most. He will never be able to prove in a scientific box that real food is better than any other choice. There will never be consensus on who determines the ideal outcome for a person, so the argument is permanent. And if your position is that anyone without studies does not belong in the conversation, he accepts that as fair.

What he offers instead is a subtraction. Take away the white jacket, the computer, the spreadsheet, the lab, the microscope, and remember that at one time none of it existed. How were people moving forward? Traveling across land? Reproducing and nurturing a child? Living past thirty? Sustaining day to day? If that is not enough, watch Alone or Naked and Afraid. Indirect empirical evidence is being captured right now, with no computer and no lab. Eat the squirrel. Forage some fiber off the bark.

What sits over all of it is the modern assumption that measurement and analysis are inherently good and right. Carl named it the optimization mindset. James put the real starting point somewhere else: the evolutionary mismatch. Do not open with modern times and phones and therefore. Open with what the mismatch actually is, then have the conversation.

### Nobody Gets Addicted to Chicken Breast

Carl brought in Dopamine Nation, which he described as mostly confirming what you already know. Make a drug more potent and more available and people get addicted. Put a device in front of a natural desire and people get addicted. The simplest definition of addiction is overconsuming or overdoing something that is not good for you or those around you, which means anything can qualify, including learning and reading.

Then the empirical point. People do not become addicted to chicken breast. Nobody is so addicted to broccoli they cannot stop. But reach into the bag with fifteen to eighty five ingredients and something in there tricks the brain into loving what it is getting while never satisfying the deeper signal that says you are good, stop. Pleasure and pain occur in the same place in the brain, so they have to be balanced on purpose. That is where earning your dopamine comes from, and James noted that high intensity exercise carries the same pleasure load, which is why it is just as hard to pull people away from.

### To What End

Before any of this becomes a script, Carl put the other half of the conversation on the table. If the hockey player says he wants to be as lean as humanly possible, full stop. Eating real food is not the fastest route to maximum leanness, and it is not honest to pretend otherwise. So the coach has a choice. Jockey for position and try to change the person's aim, or say plainly that eating real food is a good practice and let it stand. Either way, to what end belongs in the conversation.

### Truth Two: Rhythm and Timing, Which Are Not the Same Thing

Asked when to eat, James gave the public answer first: daylight hours. You should be able to see what you are doing. To a coach, the same answer becomes a time when the body can actually digest and break down the food.

Next comes hunger and satiety, which is what parents and grandparents already said. Eat when you are hungry. Alongside it sit the food hygiene practices that used to be built into the day without anyone naming them. You sat down. You were relaxed. You were probably talking to someone. You knew by intuition when you were satisfied. Nothing was measured and nothing was wasted, so the portion was probably smaller than what lands on a plate today.

Carl separated the two words. Rhythm is consistency. Timing is when you are consistent. For a nurse working night shifts, timing is going to be less than optimal, so you coach rhythm and you tell the truth about the environment. James told someone close to them exactly that within weeks of meeting her: get out of your job, your job is going to kill you. Otherwise you are applying band-aids. Fake lights at three in the morning while you chew organic beef.

The generalized version he would offer the hockey player is three square meals a day. Breakfast, lunch, and dinner is not a sociological experiment. It emerged because people wake up hungry, get hungry again in the middle of the day, and sit down at the end of it. All the principles ride inside that one simple instruction, and when the person comes back having heard about two meals a day, you have somewhere to start that is not the sports medicine paradigm.

That is the other rule for applying any of this. Do not hand a client a sports medicine answer. Find the inroad. Do you have fifteen minutes to find a corner in the basement of the hospital, sit down, relax, and chew on some meat? Yes. Then that is where we start.

### Hunger Is Not the Signal It Used to Be

Carl's caveat: eat when you are hungry does not work for most people anymore. They need to be rehabbed out of their hunger signals first. James agreed and named a cause. Hunger is difficult today because people are doing useless intense activity. Take that out and things change. Both of them have watched it happen during fasting experiments, through injury layoffs, and in a polar style of training, meaning real absolute strength work paired with easy aerobic work rather than anything exhaustive. The observation was the same each time. I do not require as much sugar. Hunger is not a nutrition-only variable. Movement moves it, and so does behavior, including anxiety and the pursuit of pleasure.

### Respect the Digestive Process

Carl walked the mechanics deliberately. You salivate and the body produces gastric juice before food arrives. You chew, which is mechanical work, and enzymes come with it. Waves of muscle contraction move it down. Two to six hours in the stomach, then the small intestine, then the large intestine, then out. The whole process takes energy the body has to have.

Respect that and other decisions follow on their own. Maybe you do not do that type of session at that time, because it interferes. You certainly do not want digestion running while you are asleep, because your system has other work to do overnight to get you ready to do it again tomorrow. This is not precision at six oh six. It is rhythm, so the body knows when to prepare. It is also why sleep, food, and movement have to be considered together. When one of the three goes, the other two get hit, and you end up in a loop: sleep is poor, satiety is offset, digestion is compromised, food choices get worse, sleep is poor.

Carl's summary prescription is the strongest line in the episode. Find rhythm and time in which you eat, without even talking about what you are eating. If you sleep at nine, stop eating around five thirty or six, then find two other openings in the day. Much of the rest sorts itself out, because most people are not sitting down at noon to crush ice cream, especially when they know it is one of three meals that day. Do that and you never have to get to whether it was 110 grams of protein.

### There Is No Third Truth

Carl said outright that he did not want to dig into a third one, because so many of the rabbit holes are downstream of those two. James added the one thing missing from the chart: awareness and intuition. They are not written into the code under the heading of nutrition, and they do not need to be. They live inside the practice of eating real food with rhythm and timing. The goal is to synchronize behavior, exercise, and nutrition, not to read every journal under the sun figuring out how to optimize in order to synchronize.

This is also why James defends the demarcation and the years of reading. It takes an entire practice of fitness coaching to realize that much of what you learned was futile, and realizing it is what lets you deliver the basic truthful principles with more steam behind them. Starting out you say lunges and broccoli and be kind because you believe it, without fully knowing why. As a craftsman you say your own version. As a master you say the same thing again, except now there are fifty books underneath it.

The highest leverage version is teaching it early. Give a young person intuition, awareness, and knowledge and they are laughing at twenty five when someone tries to sell them high intensity activity and a supplement stack, because it does not abide by anything they learned at seven. Carl has had that conversation with his son about chewing his food, and why. James called it an open runway to someone's maximal potential.

### Exercise Is the Great Assessor

Carl closed with the line he has used for years, and named it as his bias. Exercise and movement is the great assessor. It tells you what is off in the basic lifestyle guidelines. Go do something genuinely challenging and you will know intuitively whether your sleep was good, whether you are eating well, whether you have seen the sun, whether your head is clear. Can you do this thing and recover from it? If not, let us find out what is going on.

James's bias runs the same direction. Sleep is not on the list of three things in his house. Challenge the body is first, and his position is that everything else falls into place after that. Which reframes the sleep conversation entirely. Not are you getting enough. What are you doing that requires sleep?

Or, as they put it at the end, a small t truth they already knew. The iron never lies.
