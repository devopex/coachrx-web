#### **Addressing Push-Pull Imbalances: A Coach's Guide to Shoulder Health and Performance**

As fitness coaches, we've all encountered clients with noticeable strength disparities between pushing and pulling movements. These imbalances can significantly impact performance, movement quality, and long-term shoulder health. When a client uses the same weight on both sides despite visible asymmetries, it presents a complex coaching challenge that requires systematic assessment and thoughtful programming.

James FitzGerald of OPEX Fitness recently addressed this common coaching dilemma in his AMA series, providing valuable insights that align with what successful coaches implement in practice. His perspective emphasizes individualized assessment and the fundamental importance of push-pull balance for shoulder health and injury prevention.

#### **Why Push-Pull Balance Matters in Program Design**

Experienced coaches recognize that pushing and pulling represent opposing movement patterns that should generally maintain relative balance for optimal function. This equilibrium becomes particularly critical around the shoulder complex, where imbalances frequently manifest as pain, dysfunction, or eventually, injury.

As FitzGerald emphasizes, "We want that balance regardless of ability over time for safety of the shoulder." This statement reflects what many of us have observed in our coaching practices—clients with significant push-pull imbalances tend to develop shoulder issues more frequently than those with balanced strength profiles.

While sport-specific training may sometimes intentionally create certain imbalances, general fitness programming should maintain conscious awareness of push-pull ratios. Even when prioritizing movement quality over pure strength metrics, local muscular balance remains a foundational consideration.

#### **Practical Assessment Strategies**

Before implementing corrective programming, effective coaches utilize systematic assessment procedures to identify specific imbalances. These assessments provide objective data upon which to base programming decisions.

**Isometric Position Assessments**

Plank variations offer valuable diagnostic information about shoulder stability and symmetry:

- **Front Plank**: Observe scapular positioning and the ability to maintain active protraction
- **Reverse Plank**: Assess thoracic extension capability and scapular retraction
- **Side Plank**: Evaluate lateral stability and side-to-side differences in endurance

When administering these assessments, pay particular attention to compensatory patterns. A client's ability to maintain position with proper scapular positioning provides valuable insights into functional shoulder strength and potential imbalances.

**Range of Motion Assessments**

The active range-of-motion scratch test offers additional diagnostic information:

- Observe side-to-side differences in reaching capability
- Note compensatory patterns during reaching movements
- Document baseline measures to track improvements over time

**Dynamic Movement Assessment**

If static and range-of-motion assessments reveal potential imbalances, dynamic testing provides further confirmation:

- **Single-arm dumbbell rows**: Assess pulling strength and unilateral differences
- **Single-arm chest press**: Evaluate pushing strength and stabilization capability
- **Single-arm shoulder press**: Observe overhead stability and strength discrepancies

#### **Programming Solutions for Imbalance Correction**

Once assessment reveals specific imbalances, strategic programming becomes essential. The simplest and most effective approach involves:

1. Establishing baseline loads with the weaker limb
2. Matching volume and load between sides (even though the stronger side could handle more)
3. Maintaining this approach until balance improves
4. Gradually incorporating bilateral movements as symmetry develops

This balanced approach prevents further development of compensatory patterns while allowing the weaker side to catch up through consistent training.

#### **Recognizing Professional Boundaries**

Responsible coaches understand when a client's imbalances exceed their scope of practice. Some asymmetries stem from neurological factors, chronic adaptations to specific activities (like the "violinist example" FitzGerald mentions), or previous injuries that may require specialized intervention.

Even when referring clients to appropriate specialists, coaches maintain their role in providing balanced programming that promotes symmetrical development. The goal isn't always perfect symmetry, but rather functional balance that supports movement quality and reduces injury risk.
