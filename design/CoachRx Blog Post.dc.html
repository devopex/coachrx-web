Every coach has had the conversation. A client cancels, and the reason they give is vague enough that you can't argue with it. Life got busy. Money got tight. They want to take a break and come back in the fall. You go back through their file afterward and the training was fine, the compliance was fine, and the last real conversation you had with them was six weeks ago.

That gap is the thing to design around. Not the program.

Why relationship work needs a number

Coaches measure training closely and measure the relationship not at all. It gets treated as a quality you either have or don't, which makes it invisible right up until the point it costs you a client. Anything you can count, you can catch early.

A communication goal in CoachRx is deliberately simple: how many meaningful contacts should a client get in a week. Set it at the organization level, per coach, or for a single client who needs more attention than the rest of your roster. Every comment on a workout, every message, and every logged consultation counts against it.

The clients who leave are almost never the ones you spoke to on Tuesday. They're the ones you meant to speak to three Tuesdays ago.

What counts as a touchpoint

  • A comment on a completed workout, including a reply to something the client wrote.
  • A direct message, voice note, or Loom video sent to that client.
  • A logged consultation, whether it happened in person or over a call.
  • A check-in you actually responded to. An unanswered check-in doesn't count, by design.

The last one matters more than it looks. A check-in form that a client fills in and never hears back on is worse than no check-in at all, because you've asked them to do work and then shown them it didn't matter.

Reading the week, not the month

Each client carries a weekly status: reached, or not yet. It resets Monday. That reset is the whole mechanism — a monthly view lets you feel productive while three people quietly go a month without hearing from you, and a weekly view puts the same information in front of you while there is still week left to fix it.

In practice most coaches work it backwards. Open the roster on Thursday, filter to not yet reached, and work down the list. It takes twenty minutes and it is the highest-leverage twenty minutes in the week.

Pairing touchpoints with compliance

Touchpoints tell you who you've spoken to. Compliance tells you who needs speaking to. Read together they sort your roster into four groups, and only one of them is an emergency.

  • High compliance, recently contacted. Leave them alone. They're fine.
  • High compliance, not contacted. Low risk, but this is where you lose good clients to a competitor who calls them.
  • Dropping compliance, recently contacted. You already know. Keep working it.
  • Dropping compliance, not contacted. This is the one. Today.

Choosing the right tool for the message

Text is the least of what you can send, and most coaches over-use it because it's the fastest. The tool should match what the message is doing.

Video, when you need to show something

A Loom recording sits inside the client's file next to the workout it's about. Screen-share their logged results and talk through what you're seeing, or film thirty seconds of the correction you want on their squat. Either one lands better than a paragraph describing it.

Voice, when tone is the message

A voice note on a hard week does something text cannot. Clients read flat text as disappointment more often than you'd think, and a fifteen-second recording removes the ambiguity entirely.

Scheduled messages, when timing is the point

Write it now, send it Sunday night. Useful for the message you know you'll want to send at a moment you know you'll be busy — the morning of a competition, the first day of a new phase, the week you're travelling.

Making it a habit instead of a project

None of this works as a one-off audit. The coaches who hold retention above ninety percent do a version of the same small routine, and it fits in the gaps of a normal week.

  • Monday: set the week's goal and glance at anyone flagged in Needs Attention.
  • Midweek: clear check-in responses, and reply to every one of them.
  • Thursday: filter to not-yet-reached and close the gap.
  • Monthly: look at the touchpoint trend per coach, not per client, and see whether the standard is holding as the roster grows.

The last one is what separates a practice from a job. When you add a second coach, the relationship standard is the thing that quietly drifts, because it lives in habits rather than in the program document. A number makes it teachable.

Set your communication goal this week, then check on Thursday how many clients you'd have missed. That gap is your retention risk, and it's the first thing worth fixing.
